Build a tiny world in a jar.

This site shows you how to make a living terrarium — plants, soil, and helpful little creatures working together like a mini forest.

Let's begin Weekly checklist

1 What is a terrarium?

A terrarium is a little garden in a glass container. The plants, soil, water and tiny animals make a mini ecosystem. That means they help each other, a bit like a woodland floor in real life.

Closed terrarium

This one has a lid. Water rises, turns into tiny droplets on the glass, then rains back down. It is great for moss, ferns and rainforest plants.

Open terrarium

This one has no lid, or the lid stays off. Air moves more freely and it stays drier. Good for plants that dislike soggy soil.

Bioactive terrarium

This is a living team. Plants grow, and a clean-up crew of woodlice and springtails eats leftover bits so mould does not take over.

This guide is about a bioactive tropical terrarium — the fun, living kind. You and a grown-up can build it together.

2 Choosing a container

Clear glass is best, because light needs to reach the plants. Bigger jars are easier than tiny ones. Creatures and roots like a bit of space.

Good choices

  • A large glass jar with a lid
  • A small aquarium or sweet jar
  • A vase you can still reach into
  • Smooth sides, so you can watch the world inside

Things to check

  • Wash it with warm water and dry it well
  • Look for cracks — glass can break
  • A lid is handy for a closed, humid home
  • Ask a grown-up before you move a heavy jar

Start with a container at least as wide as a dinner plate if you can. A wide jar is much easier to plant and to look after.

3 Layers, step by step

Think of the jar like a tiny hillside. Each layer has a job. Build from the bottom up, and do not rush.

1

Drainage

Add 3 to 5 cm of clay balls (LECA) or clean gravel. This pocket at the bottom holds spare water so roots do not sit in a puddle.

2

Mesh barrier

Lay a piece of mesh, pond netting or landscape fabric over the stones. A thin layer of sphagnum moss also works. This stops the soil tumbling down into the drainage.

3

Substrate / soil mix

Add a fluffy tropical mix: coco coir, a little house-plant compost, orchid bark and some sphagnum moss. A pinch of horticultural charcoal helps keep the soil fresh. Aim for 6 to 10 cm of soil if the jar is big enough.

4

Leaf litter

Scatter dry, clean leaves on top — oak or beech leaves are excellent. This is food and cover for your clean-up crew.

5

Moss, wood and hides

Press sheets of moss onto the soil. Add a piece of cork bark, a safe stick, or a small bit of rotting hardwood. Critters love dim hidey-holes.

Do not use soil dug straight from the garden. It can hold slugs, mystery insects or plant food that is too strong for a jar.

4 Plants that work well

Pick small plants that enjoy damp air and bright shade. Leave space between them so they can grow. Trim a stem if it gets too long — that is normal and kind, not mean.

Fittonia

Nerve plant. Pretty veined leaves. Loves humidity and will droop if it is too dry — a drink usually perks it up.

Ferns

Lemon button fern and other small ferns love a closed jar. Maidenhair ferns are prettier, but a bit fussier.

Moss

Cushion moss and sheet moss make a soft green carpet. Keep them gently moist, never bone dry.

Pothos

A tough trailing plant. It grows quickly, so snip it back when it starts to take over the jar.

Peperomia

Thick, cheerful leaves and a compact shape. A brilliant beginner plant for a humid jar.

Also lovely

Small pilea, syngonium (arrowhead vine) and mini philodendron all settle in well. Skip succulents — they dislike a steamy closed jar.

5 Creatures and the clean-up crew

These animals are not just pets. They are workers. They eat decaying leaves and leftover food, which helps stop mould. Tap a card to flip it and read more.

Who lives together?

The dream team is springtails plus isopods. Add millipedes only if the jar is roomy and there is extra food. Never mix predators with the clean-up crew.

Safe handling

Wash your hands before and after. Pick creatures up gently, or scoop them with a soft brush. Never squeeze. If you are unsure, just watch them in the jar — that is still brilliant exploring.

A careful note

Centipedes are not millipedes. Centipedes have one pair of legs on each segment and they hunt. Leave them out of a friendly planted jar.

6 Water, light, air and food

Watering and moisture

Use a spray bottle. Rainwater or cooled boiled water is kinder than very hard tap water.

  • A little soft condensation on the glass in the morning is perfect.
  • If the glass is always dripping and the soil looks soggy, it is too wet. Take the lid off for a while.
  • If there is no mist and the soil is pale and dry, give a light spray and close the lid.
  • The soil should feel like a wrung-out sponge, not a swamp.

Light

Give the jar bright, indirect light. Near a window is good, but not in the sunbeams.

Direct sun can turn a closed jar into a tiny oven and harm plants and animals. If the room is dim, a small LED grow light for about 10 to 12 hours a day works well. Ask a grown-up to help set that up.

Ventilation

Even a closed terrarium likes a breath of fresh air. Open the lid for 10 to 30 minutes, a few times a week.

This keeps the air from going stale and helps stop extra mould. If you ever smell a sour, rotten-egg smell, open the lid straight away and check for soggy soil or leftover food.

Feeding

The main food is leaf litter. Top it up when the old leaves disappear.

  • Tiny pieces of cucumber or carrot make a treat.
  • A flake of fish food or a pinch of dried protein once in a while is enough.
  • Put food on a leaf or bottle cap, not buried in the soil.
  • Take leftover or mouldy food out after a day or two.

7 Common problems and fixes

Do not panic if something looks odd. Terrariums tell you what they need, if you look closely.

Mould

White fluff often means the jar is too wet, too still, or has leftover food. Remove the mouldy bit, open the lid, ease off the spraying, and let springtails get to work.

Overwatering

Soggy soil and constant drips are the clues. Leave the lid off, and you can gently press a paper towel onto puddles. Next time, mist less.

Too dry

Moss turns crispy and fittonia may flop. Mist the sides and soil, then close the lid so the moisture can cycle.

Pests

Fungus gnats like wet soil — let the top dry a little. Tiny pale mites often arrive after too much food; feed less and they usually fade. Ask a grown-up before using any spray.

Plants dying

Yellow, mushy leaves often mean rot from too much water. Pale, stretching plants may need more light, but still not direct sun. Take out dead leaves so they do not rot in the jar.

Bad smells

A fresh woodland smell is fine. A sour or rotten smell is not. Open the jar, remove rotting food or leaves, and let it dry a little. If it still stinks, the soil may need rebuilding with a grown-up's help.

10 Safety first

Please remember

  • Ask a grown-up before you start, and when you move glass or use tools.
  • Wash your hands before and after you work in the jar.
  • Do not take creatures from nature reserves or other protected places.
  • If you borrowed local woodlice, put them back where you found them if you stop keeping them.
  • Do not release shop-bought or non-native animals into the wild.

You have got this

Look after the jar a little each week, and it will look after itself in between. Watch closely, change one thing at a time, and enjoy your tiny jungle.
